ML-Driven Creator Recommendations

I turned a creator-first directory into a content-first, ML-ranked matching surface for partnership ads, one of Instagram’s highest-impact formats.

What I owned

The brand-side recommendation surface end to end — hierarchy, the creator card, the query model, and the feedback controls that trained ranking.

What I bet on

Brands were already judging creators through content. Rank on ad performance signal, and lead with the content itself.

What I cut

Free-text AI search — approved by leadership, then sequenced out of phase 1 by me.

Problem

Brands were evaluating creators on the wrong unit.

The old surface put a creator’s bio, handle, and follower count front and center, and minimized their content. But that isn’t how the decision actually got made: brands were scanning content, using it to guess whether a creator could carry an ad. In paid marketing, brands rely on niche creators for authentic, high-performing content — but 77% of searches returned no matches, and agencies spent up to 1.5 months sourcing creators by hand.

Cost to brands

Every candidate meant leaving the surface to look at the creator’s profile individually. Evaluation was slow, subjective, and inconsistent across a team.

Cost to creators

Reach was the thing that got you seen. Creators whose content converted but whose following was mid-sized rarely surfaced at all.

1

Creator content buried in the layout

For paid marketing, content quality matters. But the creator card was built around creator stats like follower count, location, and category, with no way to preview the content itself, leaving brands unable to evaluate what actually drives results.

2

Manual creator sourcing

Sourcing and evaluating creators was highly manual — often outsourced entirely to third-party agencies — making it the biggest pain point in running partnership ads efficiently.

3

Lack of visibility on brand partnerships

A creator's track record partnering with brands or posting branded content is a strong signal of how likely their content is to perform as a partnership ad. But the discovery experience didn't surface this history at all, making it hard for brands to gauge the probability that a creator's content would convert into a successful partnership ad.

Follower count wasn’t the villain — it still matters, and it carried through into the redesign. The problem was its rank in the hierarchy.

The Bet

Meta already knew which creator content converts. Rank on that.

Partnership ads had been running long enough to produce real performance signal on creator content. That signal existed inside the system but never reached the surface where brands were choosing — recommendation quality was an exposure problem before it was a modeling problem. I saw an opening: use that signal to fuel the marketplace’s pivot to partnership ads, Instagram’s fastest-growing format.

Who I had to convince

ML, that a design surface could produce clean enough training signal to be worth wiring up. Product, that a content-led card wouldn’t read as a feed. Both needed to see the failure cases before the happy path.

Approach

Three shifts, and everything else followed from them.

I led cross-functional ideation across 80+ ideas, prototyped the strongest, and aligned leadership on matchmaking as the highest-leverage bet.

Decisions

Four decisions, and the versions I killed to get to them.

Each one had a defensible alternative. What follows is the rubric I judged against, what each rejected variant got wrong specifically, and what shipped.

D1

Why content-first, when the industry ranked on reach

Follower count is a proxy brands reach for because it’s legible, not because it predicts a campaign outcome. Content is the thing they’re actually assessing, so I made the content the primary object and let the ad-performance signal explain the ranking underneath it.

The alternative I rejected — list view, ranked by stats

Denser, sortable, familiar from ad tools. I pushed back and stayed with a grid: a list is hard to skim when the payload is imagery, and it pushes content into a thumbnail — straight back to the old surface’s failure. Rows optimize for comparing numbers. Brands weren’t comparing numbers.

D2

Designing the card for a two-second scan

The card is the whole product in one tile. A brand should be able to form a yes/maybe/no in about two seconds, and a grid of them should stay scannable. I established four evaluation principles and judged four variants against them.

R1

Content readable before anything else

R2

Says why this creator was recommended

R3

Uniform height, so the grid holds

R4

One decision per tile, not a browse session

V1
V1

Simplest version

Failed R2

Content plus identity, no ad metrics. Clean, and useless: nothing told a brand how this creator would move an ad goal, so the card couldn’t justify its own ranking.

V2
V2

Reels-native ratio

Failed R3

Brands care most about reels, so the card followed the reel’s aspect ratio. It made the tile far too tall — fewer creators per screen, and a page that no longer scanned as a grid.

V3
V3

Multi-content thumbnails

Failed R1 · R4

More evidence per creator, but at thumbnail size none of it was readable. The clutter cost more than the extra samples were worth, and it turned each tile into a browse task.

V4

Shipped

Held all four

One hero piece of content, one consistent ratio across posts and reels, with automated cropping so faces and subjects survive the crop, plus a dynamic line explaining why this creator was recommended.

The density answer came out of V2 and V3 together: one uniform tile is worth more than either a truer crop or more samples, because the grid — not the card — is what a brand is actually reading.

D3

I proposed AI search. I also cut it from phase 1.

A free-text box — describe the campaign, get creators — was the clearest expression of intent-driven discovery, and it had leadership buy-in. I sequenced it out anyway. An open text field promises the model understands anything you type. Before the ranking model had been trained on real marketplace behavior, it would have made a promise the results couldn’t keep, and every weak answer would have read as the whole surface being wrong.

What I gave up

The most differentiated part of the pitch, and the demo that made the vision land in a room.

What I shipped instead

Structured intent — bounded inputs the model could answer well — with search staged for when accuracy could be evidenced rather than asserted.

What would have changed my mind

Offline retrieval quality good enough on real campaign briefs to hold up without a curated demo set.

D4

Closing the loop with actions brands already take

A recommendation surface with no feedback path decays. Rather than add a rating widget nobody would use, I turned the actions brands take anyway into training signal — weighted by how much intent each one actually reveals.

Strongest signal — message the creator

Reaching out is a real commitment, so it counts for the most: it pulls similar creators forward in future recommendations.

Positive — save · show more

Shortlisting a creator says the match was right even if the campaign isn’t ready. It feeds ranking without asking for extra work.

Negative — not interested · show less

The only control whose whole job is correction. It gives a brand somewhere to put a bad match instead of losing trust in the surface.

We’ll show fewer creators like this.

The Hard Part

Designing for a model that is sometimes wrong

The happy path was the easy half. A ranked surface has to stay credible on the days the ranking is off, which meant designing three states before the default one.

1

Thin signal

A niche brief with few strong matches. The surface says so and narrows the set rather than padding it out to fill a grid.

[ low-confidence state ]
2

A wrong recommendation

Dismissal has to be one action, visibly change the next set, and never feel like it cost the brand anything to correct.

3

The creator who never surfaces

Ranking on ad performance means some eligible creators sit below the fold indefinitely. Worth naming: it’s the cost of the bet, not a bug in it.

What I Got Wrong

A Creator Can Meet Every Criterion and Still Make Content That Doesn’t Resonate

Partnership ads eligibility was the input I trusted most, and it’s a floor, not a prediction. Brands rejected creators who passed every check because the work simply wasn’t for them. Balancing eligibility against taste is the part I’d design first if I started again — and the part that decided whether the surface felt smart or generic.

Also, in order

One piece of content isn’t enough to decide on. I optimized for the two-second scan and under-served the second look.

And

Brands wanted control — sort, filter, search — not to sit and receive recommendations they could only mark “not interested.” I read the passive model as simpler; they read it as less agency.
